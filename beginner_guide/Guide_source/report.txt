Abstract
Floods change quickly, and a forecast of where the water will be in the next few hours helps
people decide what to protect and where to move. In this project we built deep learning
models that read the last few depth maps of a flood, together with the rain that has already
fallen and the height of the ground, and predict the next water depth maps. The main model
is a convolutional network followed by a convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM). We trained
and tested it on FloodCastBench, a public dataset of four simulated floods in Australia, the
UK, Pakistan and Mozambique. On top of the forecaster we added a capsule network that
rates how severe the flooding is, Grad-CAM heat maps, and written physics and severity
rules.
We judged every model against simple reference forecasts, namely copying the last map
and continuing the last change. Forecasts are scored with a skill number, where 0 means no
better than copying the last map and 1 means perfect. In a notebook that forecasts whole
maps 30 minutes ahead, our first model (Model A) beat copying in three of four tests, but
it scored 0.15 to 0.35 in those tests, where the trend forecast scored 0.91 to 0.95 (and 0.99
in the fourth). A second model (Model B) that starts from the trend matched it but did not
beat it. A larger study of about 310 training runs on a laptop, which forecasts up to 2.5
hours ahead, showed that the depth history carries almost all of the skill. A table with two
inputs per cell reached 0.74 to 0.79, a fitted linear model of the depth history reached 0.79
to 0.82, and the best networks reached 0.81 to 0.87 (this is with the project’s original input,
which includes up to 30 minutes of future rain, and with past rain only the local CNN scores
about 0.02 lower). Neighbouring pixels and a network about 140 times larger added nothing
measurable. At equal training length the ConvLSTM was no more accurate than a plain
CNN, although it was more stable when the start of a flood was hidden from training.
Longer training gave a small gain (up to 0.024) and a larger batch with a higher learning
rate a small loss (up to 0.026), which are only a few times the seed noise. We conclude that
up to 2.5 hours a simple function of the recent depths of each cell does most of the work,
and that the memory of the LSTM is still untested beyond that.
Keywords: flood forecasting, spatiotemporal forecasting, CNN-LSTM, ConvLSTM,
FloodCastBench, capsule network, Grad-CAM, baseline comparison, skill score.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background and motivation
Floods damage homes, crops and roads every year, and the harm depends strongly on how
early people are warned. Physics-based flood simulators solve the equations of water flow
over the terrain and give detailed depth maps, but simulating a large flood can take days on
a powerful GPU, as the authors of the FloodCastBench dataset report [1]. A trained deep
learning model is much cheaper to run. It can produce a forecast in seconds, so it can act
as a fast stand-in for the simulator [2, 3].

1.2 Problem statement
A flood is a sequence of maps. For each square cell of a grid we know the depth of the
water at several moments, and we want the depth at the next moment. The model receives
the last few depth maps, the rain that has already fallen, and the height of the ground. In
the Colab notebook it receives four depth maps taken 30 minutes apart, so the oldest one is
1.5 hours old, and it predicts the map 30 minutes later. In the laptop study it receives five
maps and predicts the next five, which reaches 2.5 hours ahead. In both parts the main test
is a flood the model has never seen. The notebook also scores the last 15% of the training
flood.
There is a catch. Over 30 minutes the water on most of the map hardly changes, so the
simplest forecast, “the water stays where it is”, is already very accurate. A deep model is
only useful if it does better than such simple forecasts. We therefore score every model
with a skill number. A skill of 0 means no better than copying the last map, and 1 means a
perfect forecast (the exact definition is in Section 3.3).

1.3 Objectives
   1. Build a CNN-LSTM that forecasts flood depth maps, using TensorFlow and
      Keras.
   2. Add a capsule network that rates the flood severity, Grad-CAM explanations, and
      written rules that check the network (the neuro-symbolic part of our original
      proposal).
   3. Test the models on floods they have never seen and compare them with simple
      reference forecasts.
   4. Run controlled experiments on the optimiser settings, the training length, the
      architecture and the inputs, to find out what drives the skill.
   5. Report clearly which parts worked and which did not.

2. Literature review
2.1 Machine learning for floods
Mosavi et al. reviewed machine learning methods for flood prediction [2], and Bentivoglio
et al. reviewed deep learning methods for flood mapping [3]. Together they show how
widely machine learning is now used for floods. Kratzert et al. trained LSTM networks on
rainfall and river flow records and showed that the network can learn the link between the
two directly from data [4]. Much of this work predicts river flow or maps which areas are
flooded. Our task is different, because we forecast the depth of the water on every cell of a
map.

2.2 Convolutional and recurrent networks
A convolutional neural network (CNN) slides small filters over an image, so the same
pattern detector works everywhere on the map [5]. A long short-term memory network
(LSTM) reads a sequence step by step and keeps a memory that is controlled by gates [6].
Shi et al. combined the two ideas in the ConvLSTM, where the operations inside the LSTM
are convolutions, so the memory is itself a map. They used it to forecast radar rainfall maps
[7]. PredRNN is a later model that adds a second memory path to this idea [8]. A
ConvLSTM looks like a natural choice for flood maps, because the water at one cell
depends on the water around it. Section 4.3 tests whether this helps.

2.3 Flood forecasting benchmarks
FloodCast combines satellite observations, a hydrodynamic model and a neural network
built on Fourier neural operators (FNO) to forecast floods over large regions [9, 10]. An
FNO is a network that works on whole fields at once and can be used at different map sizes.
The same group then released FloodCastBench, a dataset of four real floods that were re-
created with a physics simulator at several map sizes [1]. Its benchmark trains U-Net, an
image network with an encoder and a decoder [11], and two FNO models to forecast 19
steps from the first depth map. The benchmark does not include a recurrent model, and it
compares the models with each other rather than with simple forecasts.

2.4 ConvLSTM models in flood studies
Xiao et al. built a ConvLSTM for urban flood depth under changing rainfall patterns and
explored how well it extrapolates [12]. Xiang et al. used a ConvLSTM encoder-decoder
with attention to forecast floods with a measure of uncertainty at the scale of a river basin
[13]. These studies support the idea that convolutional-recurrent models suit flood data.
These studies work at the scale of an urban area and of a river basin, while we test on floods
from other countries.

2.5 Capsule networks, Grad-CAM and neuro-symbolic
ideas
In a capsule network every output is a small vector. Capsules in one layer vote for capsules
in the next layer, and votes that agree get more weight, which is called routing by agreement
[14]. Grad-CAM (gradient-weighted class activation mapping) uses gradients to colour the
parts of the input that mattered most for a prediction [15]. Neuro-symbolic AI combines a
neural network with written rules that people can read [16]. Theory-guided data science
adds scientific knowledge, such as conservation laws, to a learned model [17]. We use all
three ideas as parts of our system.

2.6 Simple forecasts and skill scores
Forecasters judge a model against a simple reference, and the usual reference is the naive
forecast that repeats the last observation [18]. A skill score measures how much of the
reference error a model removes [19]. Studies in other fields show why this matters.
Makridakis et al. compared statistical and machine learning methods on many time series
and found that the statistical methods were more accurate [20], and Zeng et al. showed that
a very simple linear model beat several Transformer models on long-term forecasting [21].
These results made us put simple forecasts at the centre of our comparison.

2.7 Summary and gap
Table 1 lists the main works. The flood studies above and the FloodCastBench benchmark
compare deep models with each other. We did not find a study that tests convolutional-
recurrent models on FloodCastBench and compares them with simple forecasts on floods
the models have never seen. This report does that, and it also checks which parts of the
CNN-LSTM matter.

3. Methodology
3.1 Dataset description
FloodCastBench contains four real floods that the authors re-created with a physics-based
simulator [1]. The flood maps are therefore simulated, not photographs or measurements.
The simulator solves the two-dimensional shallow water equations in a simplified form. In
plain words, it follows the basic rules of water flow: water runs downhill, rain adds water,
and rough ground such as forest slows it down. It is built from real data for each region: a
ground height map (FABDEM), a land cover map (Esri, made from Sentinel-2 images, with
classes such as water, trees, crops and buildings) and satellite rainfall (GPM-IMERG). For
each flood the dataset gives the water depth every 5 minutes, rain maps every 30 minutes,
a ground height map and a land cover map. The rain and ground files are stored on a 30 m
grid. Table 2 lists the four floods.

In the Colab notebook we trained one 30 m model on Australia and tested it on the UK, and
one 480 m model on Pakistan and tested it on Mozambique. The laptop study also used the
60 m maps of Australia and the UK. A 30 m cell and a 480 m cell behave very differently,
so the two scales are never mixed.

How the coarser maps were made
According to the dataset paper, the 60 m maps of Australia and the UK are the 30 m
simulation results resized to a coarser grid. For Pakistan, the authors shrank the terrain and
rainfall to 480 m and ran the simulation again at that size. The paper does not say which
method was used for the Mozambique 480 m maps, so we make no claim about it.

3.2 Pre-processing and inputs
    1. One map every 30 minutes. Rain is given every 30 minutes and the water barely
       moves in 5 minutes, so we keep one depth map in six.
    2. One common grid. The depth maps cover only part of the 30 m rain and ground
       files. We found where each depth map sits inside the 30 m grid and averaged the
       small cells inside each large cell. Two independent checks agreed within about
       one cell, namely rivers on the land cover map and valleys on the ground height
       map.
    3. Rain timing. We measured that rain file j holds the rain that falls during the 30
       minutes after depth map j. The rain files are in mm per hour, so half an hour of


                                                   7
      rain would raise the average depth by 0.5 times the file value. On the active steps
      of the raw files, the measured rise was 0.494 to 0.502 times the file value in all
      four floods. Over a whole flood the totals differ by up to about 5% (Section 4.2).
   4. Rain settings. A forecast may only use rain that has already fallen. The original
      loader of the project gave the last input frame up to 30 minutes of rain that had not
      yet fallen, which we call the hybrid input. We therefore test three rain settings:
      causal (past rain only), hybrid (the original input) and forced (the next five rain
      maps are also given, as if a rain forecast existed).
   5. Local ground height. Water flows because a cell is higher or lower than its
      neighbours, not because of its height above the sea. In the Colab notebook we give
      the model the height of each cell minus the average height of the surrounding 15
      by 15 cells. The laptop study used the height minus the average height of its 128
      by 128 patch.
   6. Tiles. The Australian map has over a million cells, so the models learn from small
      square pieces, which we call tiles or patches (64 by 64 cells in the Colab notebook
      and 128 by 128 in the laptop study). Because the networks only use small sliding
      windows, a trained network can afterwards run on a whole map.


3.3 Baselines and the skill score
We compare every model with simple reference forecasts. Most of them need no learning,
and the others have very few parameters.
   •   Copy the last map (also called persistence). The forecast is the last observed
       depth.
   •   Trend forecast. Let dt be the depth map at the last observed time and dt−1 the map
       30 minutes earlier. The forecast adds a fraction β of the last change to the last map
       and is never below zero:


       The single number β is fitted by least squares on the training part of the training
       flood only. It was 0.921 for the 30 m floods and 0.948 for the 480 m floods. A
       value below 1 means that the change is continued in a slightly damped way.
   •   In the laptop study we also used a fitted linear model of the depth history, linear
       models that add rain and terrain, a lookup table with two inputs (the current depth
       and the last change), and a multilayer perceptron (MLP), which is a small network
       that works cell by cell. The linear models and the MLP are given each cell’s own
       depth history, rain and height, plus a 15 by 15 average of the depth and of the last
       change around the cell. The linear models are ridge regressions, which are least-
       squares fits with a small penalty on the size of the weights. Their inputs are
       rescaled to the same spread before fitting, because otherwise the penalty would
       push the weights of the very small depth changes to zero. The lookup table uses
       nothing but the two values of the cell itself.

The main score is the skill:



                                             8
A skill of 1 is a perfect forecast, 0 means no better than copying, and a negative value
means worse than copying. A skill of 0.9 means that the forecast removes 90% of the
squared error of copying. For the typical error in centimetres we use the root mean square
error. In the laptop study the intervals are 95% block-bootstrap intervals. For these we
resampled blocks of start times or tiles many times and recomputed the score each time,
and the middle 95% of the results is the interval.

3.5 Severity branch, rules and Grad-CAM
Capsule network
The capsule head takes the ConvLSTM memory of the forecaster (kept frozen) and applies
three strided convolutions. It then forms 256 primary capsules of 8 numbers each and passes
them through three rounds of routing by agreement to four class capsules of 16 numbers
each. The length of a class capsule says how sure the network is about that class. The loss
is the margin loss of Sabour et al. [14], which pushes the length of the correct capsule above
0.9 and the others below 0.1, and rare classes get larger weights during training. In the
laptop study we also trained a plain dense output layer on the same features as a control.

Severity labels
We used two labels. The earlier laptop study labelled a patch by its deepest cell, in bins of
0.3, 1 and 2 m. That label does not separate the classes outside Australia at 30 m. The Colab
notebook labels a 64 × 64 tile by the share of its normally dry land that is under more than
30 cm of water: none (below 5%), minor (5 to 15%), moderate (15 to 30%) and severe
(30% or more). Rivers and lakes are left out. This label gives a spread of classes in
Australia, the UK and Mozambique. In Pakistan 698 of the 1,000 test tiles are severe, and
in the UK none is.

Water-balance rule
Almost all of the rain stays on the ground in these simulations, so the average rise of the
water over a map in 30 minutes should equal the rain in mm per hour times 0.5 hours, if no
water crosses the edge of the map. The rule shifts the forecast so that its total change
matches the rain of the last 30 minutes, which is the only rain that is known. Where the
water goes is still decided by the network.

Severity rules
Rule 1 sets the rating to “none” if less than 1% of the land is flooded now and in the forecast.
Rule 2 stops the rating from falling below the current level when the forecast is not less
flooded than the present and more than 1 mm per hour of rain fell in the last hour. Rule 3
replaces the capsule rating by the rating of the forecast map if the two are more than one
class apart. Every decision comes with a written reason.

Grad-CAM
Following Selvaraju et al. [15], we weight the ConvLSTM memory maps by the average
gradient of the quantity we want to explain and keep the positive part. For the forecaster
that quantity is the total predicted depth. For the capsule network it is the length of the
chosen class capsule. In the laptop study we also ran a deletion test. It removes the cells



                                              11
with the highest heat and checks that the forecast changes more than when random cells
are removed.

3.7 Experimental design
The project has two parts. The first is a larger controlled study that we ran on a laptop to
find out which parts of a CNN-LSTM really matter (experiment set II below). The second
is a Google Colab notebook with our submitted implementation, which tests Models A and
B on whole maps (experiment set I). The two parts use different models, patches and
protocols, so their numbers cannot be compared, and Models A and B were not part of the
laptop study.

Experiment set I (Colab notebook, whole maps)
Two models were trained, one per map scale. The 30 m model learned from the first 70%
of the Australian flood, used the next 15% for early stopping, and was tested on the last
15% of Australia and on the whole UK flood. The 480 m model did the same with Pakistan
and Mozambique. We compared Model A and Model B with copying, the trend forecast
and the water-balance rule, and we trained the capsule network, the severity rules and Grad-
CAM on top.



                                                  12
Experiment set II (laptop study)
Before the final runs we wrote down the protocol and six hypotheses. In plain words, they
said that (H1) the models beat copying, (H2) they beat linear baselines, (H3) the depth
history matters, (H4) recurrence matters, (H5) knowing more rain helps, and (H6) a stricter
test gives similar scores. The strict test also hides a block of time from training, not only
some tiles. The experiments were Australia to UK at 60 m (E60) and at 30 m (E30), and
Pakistan to Mozambique at 480 m (E480). Each one used three folds. In each fold, a
different third of the tiles of the training region was held out. We also used three rain
settings and several models. The models produce all five forecast maps in one pass and do
not feed their forecasts back as input.
After seeing the first results we added exploratory experiments on the optimiser setting, the
training length, the number of windows, the random seed and transfer in the reverse
direction. The large ConvLSTM was already part of the written plan. The linear baselines
were also fitted a second time with rescaled inputs, after we found that the first fit had
pushed the weights of the small depth-change inputs almost to zero. Together this made
about 310 training runs. All deviations from the plan are listed in the project log, which we
keep with the code.

4.2 The water-balance rule
Before using the rule we checked how well the data obey the water balance. Over a whole
flood the water added differed from the rain by up to about 5% (the ratio was 0.999 for
Australia, 0.946 for the UK, 1.028 for Pakistan and 1.045 for Mozambique). The dataset
paper says that water can leave freely at the map edge in three of the floods, and that the
Pakistan simulation lets the Indus River flow in at the edge of the map. In 73 training steps
of the Australian flood the average depth even fell, which rain alone cannot do. So the law
holds closely but not exactly. Figure 7 shows the check.




Figure 7: Water-balance check. Each dot is one 30-minute step. Across: rain times half an hour. Up:
how much the water rose on average. The dashed line is the law.

The rise matches the rain of the same 30 minutes with a correlation of 1.00, but the rule can
only use the rain of the last 30 minutes as its guess for the next 30 minutes, because future
rain is unknown. The rise correlates less with the rain of the previous 30 minutes (0.925 to
0.969). The rule helped Model A in every test and halved the Australian failure (−1.449 to
−0.710). On Model B it changed the score by between −0.007 and +0.001 (Table 6), and
on copying alone it gave only 0.000 to 0.142. So the rule corrected a weakness of Model A,
but it does not add skill to a good forecast. In our final system we keep it as an optional
physics check, and Model B does not need it.

With the 30-pass recipe the small ConvLSTM was ahead of the local CNN on the UK at

Why the original model failed
The original CNN-LSTM squeezes each frame through one dense layer of 128 numbers,
and its depth input was scaled by 5 m, so a change of a millimetre is a tiny number. Table 9
shows that it ignores the depth history completely, which explains its low skill.

4.7 Severity, rules and Grad-CAM
Capsule network against a plain layer
On Australia at 30 m with the depth-based label, the capsule head reached 67.8% on the
natural class mix and the dense control 60.2%, a gap of +0.076 (interval +0.051 to +0.101).
With the eight rotations and flips of each patch used as augmentation, the two reached
80.3% and 79.8%, and the gap shrank to +0.005 (−0.014 to +0.022). Both were far below
a trivial rule that thresholds the last observed frame (97.4%) or the model’s own forecast
(98.6% to 98.7%). The rule layer of the laptop study lowered the accuracy, for example
from 80.3% to 74.1%. The rule layer of the laptop study and the rules of the notebook are
different rules that are used with different labels, so their results cannot be compared
directly.

Colab results with the new label
Table 13 shows the capsule network on all four floods. The class of the forecast map alone
is right about 99% of the time, because the rating in 30 minutes is almost the same as the
rating now. The classifier is still of interest because a rating is easy for a non-expert to read,
but it adds little to the forecast map here. The rules raised the accuracy in all four floods.

4.8 Where the models fail, and limits of this study
   •   When we also hid a block of time from training (the strict split), the same-region
       score of the plain CNN on Pakistan fell from 0.430 to −6.56. Six of 480 windows,
       all at the very start of the flood, carried 98% of the error, and the failure repeated
       across five seeds and a second recipe. The ConvLSTM stayed at 0.742 and the
       lookup table at 0.66, while the per-pixel MLP also failed. Training on the small
       Mozambique flood and testing on Pakistan failed in the same way for the plain
       networks. A model that has not seen the start of a flood should not be trusted on
       one.

•   Every region has one flood, and E60 and E30 share the UK flood. So there are
    only two independent train-and-test pairs, even though our tables have many rows.
•   The floods are simulations, so the models learn to imitate the simulator. They have
    not been tested against observed flooding.
•   The Colab results are single runs. The two experiment sets also use different

    numbers cannot be compared.
•   The capsule network and the Grad-CAM pictures of the notebook were built on

5. Conclusion
We built a CNN-ConvLSTM forecaster for flood depth maps, added a capsule severity
network, Grad-CAM and written rules, and tested them on later parts of the training floods
and on floods from other countries. The main lesson is about how to judge such a model.
Model A beat the easiest reference, copying the last map, and looked successful. A second
simple reference that continues the last change was far better, and Model A scored well
below it. Model B, which starts from this trend, matched it but did not beat it.
The larger laptop study showed the same picture from another side. The depth history
carries nearly all of the skill. A fitted linear model of it reaches 0.79 to 0.82, against 0.81
to 0.87 for the best networks, and neighbouring pixels add nothing. At equal training length
the ConvLSTM was no more accurate than a plain CNN, but it was more stable when the
start of a flood was hidden, and its skill fell more slowly with the forecast time under the
30-pass recipe. A larger batch with a higher learning rate did not help, and longer training
helped a little. Differences below 0.02 are within seed noise. The capsule network did not
beat a plain layer once augmentation was used, and neither beat a trivial rule on the severity
task. The plain CNN also fails on the very start of a flood that was missing from training,
while the ConvLSTM and the lookup table did not.

Future work
The most useful next step is to forecast further ahead, for 6 to 24 hours, by feeding each
forecast back as input. This is where a trend should get worse and the memory of the LSTM
has a real chance to matter, so it is the fair test of the CNN-LSTM. We first have to decide
which rain to assume for the future steps, and the trend forecast should be carried forward
in the same way for comparison. We would also like to train Model A with only the
centimetre scaling and Model B without it, which would separate the two changes that we
made at once. Other steps are to rebuild the capsule network and Grad-CAM on Model B
and compare the capsule network with a plain layer on the new severity label at 3 to 6 hours
ahead, and to repeat the Colab runs with several seeds.
